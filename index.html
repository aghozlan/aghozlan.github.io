What a real lab shows you

The Macro View

Pour liquid nitrogen over a ceramic superconductor disc and it hisses down to 77 Kelvin — colder than any winter on Earth. Lower a magnet toward it and, below the material's critical temperature, it simply stops. Floating. Spinning gently. No strings, no trick.

This is genuinely how it looks in a school lab, filmed with nothing but a camera and a flask of LN₂. It's real, it's repeatable — and it tells you almost nothing about why it happens.

What no camera can show you

The Micro View

Above the critical temperature, electrons travel through the metal's atomic lattice alone, constantly scattering off vibrating atoms — that scattering is electrical resistance, and the energy lost becomes heat.

Below the critical temperature, something stranger happens: electrons stop moving alone. They bind into Cooper pairs, correlated across the entire lattice, gliding through it in perfect lock-step. There is nothing left to scatter off. Resistance doesn't drop — it hits exactly zero.

No microscope built has ever filmed a Cooper pair forming. A simulation is the only way to see it.

The point of the simulation

Real labs prove that something happens.
Simulations show you why.

That's the entire argument for building physics simulations — not as a replacement for the hands-on demo, but as the only lens that reaches the scale a real lab physically can't. Use the control panel to run both experiments yourself.
